The 5 Biggest Marketing Mistakes Contractors Make

Contractors waste thousands on marketing that doesn't work. Here are the 5 most common mistakes and what to do instead.

By Madeleine JolieOctober 19, 20266 min read

I've audited the marketing for about 40 contracting businesses over the past year. The same five mistakes show up almost every time, and they're costing these companies thousands of dollars a month in wasted spend and lost leads.

1. Paying for leads you never follow up on

This is the most expensive mistake and the most common. A contractor spends $2,000 a month on Google Ads, gets 50 leads, and calls maybe 20 of them within 24 hours. The other 30 leads sit in an inbox or a voicemail for days.

Each of those leads cost $40. Thirty ignored leads is $1,200 a month thrown away. Over a year that's $14,400.

The fix isn't spending more on ads. It's responding faster and following up more than once. Automated text-back for missed calls and form submissions, then a personal call within 15 minutes, then a follow-up text that evening. That sequence alone can double your close rate from the same lead volume.

2. No Google Business Profile or a neglected one

Some contractors don't have a GBP at all. Others set one up three years ago and haven't touched it since. Both situations mean you're invisible in the Map Pack, which is where most local searches end.

The Map Pack shows three businesses above all organic results. Getting into those three spots is free, and it brings the highest-intent leads in local search. A homeowner who clicks on a Map Pack result is looking for someone right now.

What neglected looks like: no photos added in the last year, no posts, 6 reviews, service categories that say "contractor" instead of your specific trade. Fixing this takes a few hours and costs nothing.

3. Spending on ads without a website that converts

I've seen contractors running $3,000 a month in Google Ads sending traffic to a site that takes 8 seconds to load on mobile, has no click-to-call button, and buries the contact form at the bottom of a page. They're buying traffic and then fumbling it at the front door.

Before you spend a dollar on ads, your website needs to do three things in under five seconds: tell the visitor what you do, show them you're in their area, and give them an obvious way to contact you. If your site can't do those three things, your ads are expensive billboards that people glance at and forget.

4. Marketing like a big company when you're a small one

Contractors see how national brands market and try to copy it. They write corporate-sounding copy about "delivering exceptional results with a commitment to quality." They use stock photos of people in hard hats shaking hands. They build websites that look like they belong to a company with 500 employees.

Homeowners hiring a local contractor want the opposite of that. They want to know who's actually showing up to their house. They want to see photos of real work you've done, read reviews from their neighbors, and talk to a person when they call.

Your marketing advantage as a small contractor is that you're a real person who does real work in a specific area. Lean into that. Put your face on your website. Take photos on every job site. Write like you talk. The polished corporate stuff works against you because it looks impersonal.

5. No system for collecting reviews

Word of mouth used to mean your neighbor told you about their plumber over the fence. Now it means Google reviews. A contractor with 150 reviews and a 4.8 rating will get picked over a better contractor with 12 reviews every single time.

The mistake isn't that contractors don't want reviews. It's that they don't have a system for asking. They finish a job, shake the customer's hand, and hope the customer goes home and writes a review. The rate on that is about 5 percent.

A text with a direct link to your Google review page, sent the day after the job, gets a response rate of 15 to 25 percent. Send a reminder two days later and it goes higher. Over a few months, you go from a handful of reviews to a hundred or more. It costs nothing but consistency.

The pattern behind all five mistakes

Every one of these comes down to the same problem: contractors are good at the work and bad at the systems around the work. The fix for each of them is a process that runs in the background. Automated follow-up for leads. Regular GBP maintenance. A site that converts. Authentic marketing. Automated review requests.

None of this is hard. It's just not what most contractors signed up to do. That's why the ones who build these systems, or hire someone to build them, end up with full calendars while their competitors wonder where the jobs went.
