What Makes a Good Contractor Website? (With Examples)

Most contractor websites look the same and convert nobody. Here's what actually makes a contractor site generate leads, with real examples.

By Madeleine JolieOctober 4, 20267 min read

I've looked at hundreds of contractor websites over the past year. Most of them have the same problems: a stock photo of a guy in a hard hat, a paragraph about "quality workmanship and customer satisfaction," and a contact form buried at the bottom of a page nobody scrolls to.

That site isn't working for you. Here's what does.

Your phone number has to be impossible to miss

A homeowner with a leaking pipe or a busted AC unit isn't going to browse your About page. They want to call someone right now. If your phone number isn't in the top right corner of every page, visible without scrolling, you're losing the easiest leads you'll ever get.

Click-to-call on mobile is mandatory. Over 70 percent of local service searches happen on a phone. If someone has to copy your number, switch apps, and paste it into their dialer, a chunk of them won't bother.

The homepage needs to answer three questions in five seconds

When someone lands on your site, they need to immediately know: 1. What you do (roofing, plumbing, HVAC, whatever) 2. Where you do it (city or service area) 3. How to contact you (phone, form, or chat)

That means your headline isn't "Welcome to Smith Contracting." It's "Roof Repair and Replacement in Tampa Bay. Call for a Free Estimate." Boring? Maybe. But the person with a leaky roof doesn't want clever. They want to know you can fix it and you're nearby.

Service pages beat a single "Services" page every time

A lot of contractors list everything they do on one page. Roof repair, new construction, gutters, siding, all crammed together with a sentence each.

That page will never rank on Google for anything specific. Google rewards pages that go deep on one topic. A dedicated page for "roof repair in Tampa" with 500 words about your process, pricing ballpark, and common problems will outrank your competitor's one-line mention of roof repair on their generic services page.

If you do five things, you need five service pages. Each one should have its own title tag, its own meta description, and enough real content to be useful to someone who's researching that specific service.

Photos of your actual work, not stock images

This is the easiest way to stand out from every other contractor in your market. Homeowners can tell the difference between a stock photo and a picture you took with your phone on a job site. The phone photo wins every time because it's proof you do the work.

Before and after photos work especially well. A rotted deck next to the new one you built. A bathroom mid-demo next to the finished tile. These are more convincing than any paragraph of copy you could write.

Take photos on every job. It takes 30 seconds. Over a few months you'll have a gallery that sells for you.

Reviews on the site, not just on Google

Your Google reviews matter for the Map Pack. But reviews on your actual website matter for conversions once someone clicks through. If a homeowner lands on your site and sees 15 five-star reviews from real people in their area, they're calling you.

A live review feed that pulls from Google works best because it updates itself. Static testimonials with no name or date look made up. A feed with real names, dates, and star ratings looks like social proof because it is.

A quote form that asks the right questions

"Name, email, message" is a terrible form for a contractor. The homeowner doesn't know what to put in the message box, and you get leads with zero useful info.

A better form asks: What service do you need? What's your zip code? When do you need it done? Add a phone number field and make it required. Now when you call that lead back, you already know what they want and you sound prepared.

Mobile speed matters more than desktop design

Your site can look incredible on a 27-inch monitor. If it takes four seconds to load on a phone over LTE, you've already lost half your visitors. Google's own data shows 53 percent of mobile visitors leave a page that takes longer than three seconds to load.

Compress your images. Skip the auto-playing video on the homepage. Test your site speed at PageSpeed Insights and aim for a mobile score above 80.

The sites that actually book jobs

The best contractor websites I've seen aren't flashy. They load fast, they make it obvious what you do and where, they have real photos and real reviews, and they make it dead simple to call or fill out a form. Everything else is decoration.
