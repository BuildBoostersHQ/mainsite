Why Most Contractor Websites Don't Generate Leads (And What to Fix)

You have a website but it's not bringing in leads. Here are the 7 most common reasons contractor websites fail and exactly how to fix each one.

By Madeleine JolieNovember 24, 20266 min read

"I have a website but it doesn't do anything for me." I hear this from contractors at least once a week. They paid someone $1,500 or $3,000 to build a site, it looks fine, and it generates zero leads.

The problem is almost never the design. It's usually one of these seven things.

1. Your site takes too long to load

Pull up your website on your phone right now. Count to three. If the page hasn't fully loaded by then, you're losing over half your visitors before they even see your content.

The usual culprits: a giant hero image that wasn't compressed, an auto-playing video on the homepage, too many scripts loading at once, or cheap shared hosting. Run your site through Google PageSpeed Insights. If your mobile score is below 60, speed is killing your conversions.

The fix is usually straightforward: compress images to under 200KB each, ditch the auto-play video, and talk to your host about upgrading if the server response time is slow.

2. No clear call to action above the fold

A homeowner lands on your site. They see your logo, a nice photo, and a tagline about quality craftsmanship. Cool. Now what? If they have to scroll to find out how to contact you, a lot of them won't bother.

Your phone number needs to be in the header on every page, clickable on mobile. A "Get a Free Estimate" button should be visible without scrolling. These aren't optional. They're the entire purpose of the site.

3. You're targeting nobody

"Smith Contracting - Quality Service You Can Trust" tells a homeowner nothing about what you do or where. Your homepage title and heading should include your trade and your location. "Roof Repair and Replacement in Tampa Bay" tells Google what to rank you for and tells the visitor they're in the right place.

If your website could belong to any contractor in any city, it's too generic to rank for anything specific.

4. One page trying to do everything

Your homepage lists all your services in a paragraph, mentions your service area in a sentence, has three reviews, and ends with a contact form. That's a site that ranks for nothing because it goes deep on nothing.

Each service needs its own page. Each major location needs its own page. Each page should target one specific keyword and go deep enough on that topic to actually be useful. Google rewards depth and specificity. A 500-word page about "drain cleaning in Fort Lauderdale" will outrank a 50-word bullet point on your services page every time.

5. No social proof on the site

Reviews on Google help your Map Pack ranking. Reviews on your website help your conversion rate once someone clicks through. If a homeowner lands on your site and sees zero reviews, they have no reason to trust you over the competitor whose site has 50 five-star testimonials.

A live Google review feed is ideal because it updates itself and looks authentic. Static testimonials with first-name-only attribution ("Great work! - John") look made up and don't help.

6. Your contact form asks the wrong questions

"Name, email, message." That's the default form on most contractor websites and it's terrible. The homeowner doesn't know what to put in the message box, so they either write a novel or leave it blank. And you get a lead with no useful information.

A better form asks: What service do you need? (dropdown or radio buttons) What's your zip code? When do you need it done? Phone number (required).

Now when you call that lead back, you already know what they want. You sound prepared. And you have their phone number to actually reach them, because email response rates from contractors are dismal.

7. Nobody knows the site exists

A website with no traffic generates no leads. If you built the site and did nothing else, the only visitors are people who already know your business name. That's maybe 5 to 10 visitors a month.

Traffic comes from three places: Google (organic rankings from SEO and content), Google Ads (paid), and referrals (links from other sites, social media, directories). If you're not actively working on at least one of these, your site is a billboard on a road nobody drives on.

The cheapest long-term traffic source is organic SEO. Blog posts, service pages, and location pages that rank for searches homeowners make. It takes three to six months to build momentum, but once it's working, the traffic is free and it compounds.

The fix is usually simpler than you think

Most contractor websites are one or two fixes away from generating leads. Speed up the site, add a clear phone number and form above the fold, build dedicated service pages, get reviews on the site, and start publishing content that ranks.

You don't need to redesign the whole thing. You need to make it do the job it was supposed to do in the first place: turn a visitor into a phone call.
