Best Photos for a Contractor Website: What to Shoot and How to Use Them

Real photos of your crew, your trucks and your finished work do more to build trust than any headline on your website. You can take every one of them with the phone in your pocket.

By Madeleine JolieNovember 24, 20266 min read
The short answer

The best photos for a contractor website are real ones: your team in uniform, your branded truck, finished jobs, before-and-after pairs shot from the same spot, and close-ups that show the quality of your work. Shoot them on your phone in good daylight with the lens wiped clean, shrink the files before uploading so the site stays fast on mobile, write a short description of each photo for alt text, and always ask the homeowner before you post pictures of their home.

Homeowners are deciding whether to let a stranger into their house, so they look closely at what your website shows them. Stock photos of smiling models in spotless hard hats don't help with that, because everyone has seen them before and they don't prove anything about your business. A picture of your actual crew on an actual job does. The good news is that you don't need a photographer to get great contractor website photos, just a plan for what to shoot and a few habits on the job.

Phone photography tips for contractors

Modern phones take photos that are more than good enough for a website. The difference between a photo that looks professional and one that looks careless usually comes down to a few simple habits:

  • Wipe the lens first. A phone that lives in a work pocket gets a film of dust and fingerprints, which makes every photo look hazy.
  • Use daylight. Shoot outdoor jobs in the morning or late afternoon, and turn on the room lights and open the blinds for indoor jobs.
  • Hold the phone horizontally for website photos, since most website sections are wider than they are tall.
  • Clean up the scene. Move ladders, buckets, trash and cords out of the frame before you shoot the finished job.
  • Keep it straight. Turn on the grid lines in your camera settings and line up walls and rooflines with them.
  • Skip heavy filters. Homeowners want to see what the work really looks like, so a little brightening is fine but dramatic filters aren't.

How to take great before-and-after photos

Before-and-after photos are some of the most convincing content a contractor can have, because they show the result instead of describing it. The trick is consistency. Take the before photo the moment you arrive, then stand in that same spot, at the same height and with the same zoom, when the job is done. A small piece of tape on the floor makes that spot easy to find again. Shoot both photos in the same direction and, if possible, at a similar time of day so the light matches. On your website, put the pair side by side or one above the other on mobile, and add a short caption that says what you did and the town where you did it.

File size and page speed

A photo straight off your phone can be several megabytes, which is far bigger than a website needs. If you upload a dozen of them, your pages slow down, and the Think with Google research above shows how many mobile visitors leave when a page takes more than 3 seconds. Before you upload, resize photos so the long side is around 1,600 to 2,000 pixels for big hero images and around 1,200 pixels for gallery photos. Then save them as WebP or a compressed JPEG, aiming for well under 300 KB each. Free tools like Squoosh can do this in your browser, and many website builders compress images for you when you upload. Alt text basics

Alt text is a short written description attached to each photo. Screen readers read it aloud for people who can't see the image, and search engines use it to understand what the photo shows. Write it the way you'd describe the photo to someone over the phone, in one plain sentence.

Getting customer permission for photos of their home

A customer's house is personal, so ask before you post photos of it. The easiest way is to add a line to your estimate or contract saying you may photograph the work for your website and social media, with a box they can check if they'd rather you didn't. You can also simply ask when the job is done, which is a good moment to ask for a review too. Either way, follow a few rules:

  • No house numbers or street signs in the frame, unless the homeowner is fine with it.
  • No people, kids or pets from the household without their clear okay.
  • No personal items like family photos, mail, or anything with a name on it.
  • No license plates on cars in the driveway.

Respecting their privacy makes customers more comfortable saying yes, and it's the kind of care other homeowners notice.

The bottom line

You already have the best photos for your contractor website, or you will after a few more jobs. Shoot your crew, your truck and your finished work in good light, keep the files small, describe each one in plain words, and ask before you share someone's home. Can I use stock photos on my contractor website?

You can use a few for backgrounds while you build up your own, but real photos of your crew and your work build much more trust. Replace stock photos with your own as soon as you have them.

Do I need a professional photographer?

You don't need one to get started. A modern phone, good daylight and a clean lens are enough for great website photos, though a one-time shoot for your team and trucks can be worth it if you want a polished homepage.

How big should photos be on a contractor website?

Aim for around 1,600 to 2,000 pixels on the long side for large hero images and around 1,200 pixels for gallery photos, saved as WebP or compressed JPEG and well under 300 KB each so pages stay fast on mobile.

Do I need permission to post photos of a customer's home?

You should always get it. Add a photo line to your estimate or contract, or ask when the job is done, and keep house numbers, people and personal items out of the frame.
