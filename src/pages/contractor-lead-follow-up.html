How to Follow Up With Leads Without Being Annoying

Most contractors follow up once and give up. Here's a follow-up sequence that books more jobs without annoying potential customers.

By Madeleine JolieNovember 3, 2026Updated November 24, 20265 min read

There's a sweet spot between "I called once and left a voicemail" and "I've called this person seven times in two days." Most contractors land on the first side and lose jobs because of it.

Speed and persistence both matter, and the research on response time is clear about how fast interest fades once someone reaches out and hears nothing back.

Why contractors are bad at follow-up

You're busy. You're on a job site. You got the lead notification at 10 AM and by the time you're done working at 5 PM, you've got six leads from today that you haven't contacted, plus three from yesterday you meant to call back. It piles up fast.

Then there's the mindset thing. "If they wanted to hire me, they'd call back." That's true for about 20 percent of your leads. The other 80 percent got busy, forgot, got distracted, or are waiting to see who follows up. The contractor who stays in touch without being pushy gets those jobs.

A follow-up sequence that works

Day 1 (within 2 minutes): Automated text. "Hey, this is [name] from [company]. Got your request for [service]. When's a good time for a quick call?"

Day 1 (within 15 minutes): Call them. If no answer, leave a short voicemail and immediately send another text: "Just left you a voicemail. Feel free to text back if that's easier."

Day 1 (evening): If no response, one more text. "Hey [name], still want to get you that estimate. I'm available tomorrow morning or afternoon. What works?"

Day 3: Text. "Following up on your [service] request. We can usually get out for an estimate within a day or two. Want me to get you on the schedule?"

Day 5: Text. "Hey [name], last check-in on this. If the timing isn't right, no worries. Just reply STOP and I won't bug you."

Day 10: One final text. "Hi [name], we're running a backlog right now but I wanted to circle back on your [service] request from last week. If you still need it done, happy to fit you in."

After that, they go into a monthly nurture list where they get one text a month with a seasonal tip or offer. That's not follow-up anymore. That's brand awareness for when they're ready.

Why texting works better than calling for follow-up

You're not a telemarketer. You shouldn't feel like one. The beauty of texting for follow-up is that it's low-pressure on both sides. The lead reads it when they have a minute. They reply when they're ready. Nobody's put on the spot.

Texting also creates a written thread. The lead can scroll back and see your name, your company, and what you're offering. A voicemail disappears into a notification they'll swipe away.

The response rate on follow-up texts is roughly 45 percent. Follow-up calls get maybe 15 percent. The math makes the decision for you.

What "annoying" actually means

Five touchpoints over 10 days isn't annoying. It's professional. The homeowner submitted a quote request. They want to hear from someone. What's annoying is calling three times in one afternoon, or sending a text every day for a week.

The difference between persistent and annoying is timing and tone. Space it out. Make each message useful or relevant. Give them an easy way to opt out ("reply STOP"). If they haven't responded after five or six touches over 10 days, back off. They either went with someone else or they're not ready yet.

Automate the first three touches

You don't need to remember to do any of this manually. The first text, the follow-up text, and the Day 3 text can all be automated. You personally should make the phone call because that's where you build rapport. But the texts run on their own and keep the conversation alive while you're on a job site.

The contractors who automate this sequence consistently book 20 to 30 percent more jobs from the same lead volume. That's not more leads. That's more jobs from leads you're already paying for.
