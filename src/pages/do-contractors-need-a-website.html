Do Contractors Need a Website in 2027?

Plenty of contractors get by on referrals and a Facebook page. Here's why a website still matters in 2027, what one really costs, and the few cases where you can skip it for now.

By Madeleine JolieOctober 13, 2026Updated November 24, 20266 min read
The short answer

If you want steady work beyond your own circle of referrals, you do need a website in 2027. Homeowners look you up on their phone before they call, even when a neighbor gave them your name, and a simple site with your services, your towns, real photos, your reviews and a tap-to-call button is what turns that search into a phone call. A Facebook page or a Google Business Profile helps, but neither one replaces a site you own. The only real exception is a one-person shop that already has more referral work than it can handle.

One of the most common things contractors say about websites is that all their work comes from referrals and word of mouth, so there's no reason to pay for one. If you're booked solid for the next six months from referrals alone, that's a fair point, but there are a few good reasons to look at it differently before you decide.

Referrals look you up online too

Even when someone gets your name from a neighbor, the first thing they usually do is search for you on their phone. If nothing comes up, or all they find is a Facebook page with a last post from two years ago, some of them will keep scrolling and call a competitor who looks more established.

A website doesn't replace referrals, because what it really does is make them stick. When a homeowner searches your company name and finds a clean site with your services, photos of your actual work and your Google reviews, they have every reason to pick up the phone. Without one, some of those referrals quietly go elsewhere, and you never find out because they simply never called.

Homeowners decide fast, and on their phone

Local searches move quickly. In BrightLocal's 2026 consumer research, 72% of people looked at three or fewer businesses before deciding, and 75% made up their mind in under 30 minutes. That means you get one short look, usually on a phone screen, and your site has to show what you do, where you work and how to reach you before they move on to the next name.

Facebook isn't a website

A Facebook business page is free and easy to set up, which is a good reason to have one, but it makes a weak replacement for a real site. You don't control it, since Facebook decides who sees your posts, and a Facebook page rarely shows up when someone searches for a specific service in your town. It also leaves out everyone who doesn't use Facebook, and it gives you very little room for quote forms, booking or a page for each service you offer.

Keep the page as a place to share job photos and stay in touch with past customers. As your only home online, though, it can make a homeowner wonder whether you're an established business, and that small doubt is often enough to send them to the next contractor.

Google is where homeowners find contractors now

When someone's water heater breaks at 6 AM, they grab their phone and search something like "plumber near me" or "water heater repair" plus their city. BrightLocal found that 42% of searchers click on the Google map results, which is why a Google Business Profile matters so much, and the good news is that it's free.

Your website and your profile work best together. Google says local results are based mainly on relevance, distance and prominence, and a website with a page for each service and each town you cover gives Google much more to go on when it works out what your business does and where. That's also why a one-page site usually isn't enough.

What a contractor website actually costs

A lot of contractors get nervous here because they've heard quotes of $5,000 or more for a custom site, and agency prices really can run that high. WebFX puts small agencies at $6,000 to $12,000 for a small-business site.

You don't need a custom masterpiece, though. What you need is a site that loads fast on a phone, says what you do and where, keeps your phone number in easy reach, shows your reviews and your work, and makes it easy to ask for a quote. You can build that yourself on a website builder for roughly $20 to $50 a month, pay a freelancer for a one-time build, or go with a monthly plan where someone else handles everything. The better question isn't whether you can afford a website, because the real question is whether you can afford the calls you're missing without one.

When you can skip the website for now

If you're a one-person operation doing side jobs on weekends and your personal network keeps you busy, a website genuinely isn't a priority yet, and your money is better spent on tools and materials.

Once you want to grow, hire help, fill your calendar every week or stop depending on who happens to know you, a website becomes the foundation that everything else builds on. Google Ads, your Google Business Profile, review requests and follow-up texts all work better when you have a place to send people.

The bottom line

For almost every contractor who wants more work in 2027, a website is worth having. It doesn't need to be fancy or expensive, but it does need to be yours, load fast on a phone and make it easy for a homeowner to call you. Do I need a website if I get all my work from referrals?

If you already have more referral work than you can handle, you can wait. If you want to grow, a website helps because referred customers usually look you up before they call, and a good site gives them a reason to follow through.

Is a Google Business Profile enough without a website?

A Google Business Profile is free and important, and it's often the first thing a homeowner sees in the map results. It works best alongside a website, because your site gives Google and homeowners more detail about each service and town you cover.

Can I use my Facebook page instead of a website?

You can keep using it, but it shouldn't be your only presence online. You don't control who sees your posts, it rarely shows up in Google searches for your services, and people without Facebook have a hard time using it.
