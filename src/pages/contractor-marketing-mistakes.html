The 5 Biggest Marketing Mistakes Contractors Make

Contractors waste thousands on marketing that doesn't work. Here are the 5 most common mistakes and what to do instead.

By Madeleine JolieOctober 19, 2026Updated November 24, 20266 min read
The short answer

The five biggest marketing mistakes contractors make are paying for calls and quote requests they never follow up on, neglecting their Google Business Profile, running ads to a website that doesn't turn visitors into calls, marketing like a big faceless company, and having no system for collecting reviews. Each one has a simple fix, and every fix is a process that runs in the background so nothing depends on you remembering.

The same five marketing mistakes show up again and again in contracting businesses, and they can cost thousands of dollars a month in wasted spend and lost work.

1. Paying for leads you never follow up on

This is the most expensive mistake and the most common. Say you spend $2,000 a month on Google Ads, get 50 requests, and call maybe 20 of them within 24 hours. The other 30 sit in an inbox or a voicemail for days.

Each of those leads cost $40. Thirty ignored leads is $1,200 a month thrown away. Over a year that's $14,400.

The fix isn't spending more on ads. It's speed-to-lead, which means how quickly you respond after someone reaches out, plus following up more than once. Start with an automatic text for missed calls and form submissions, then make a personal call within 15 minutes, then send a follow-up text that evening. That sequence alone can win back a lot of the jobs that were slipping away.

2. No Google Business Profile or a neglected one

Some contractors don't have a GBP at all. Others set one up three years ago and haven't touched it since. Both situations mean you're invisible in the Map Pack, and BrightLocal found that 42% of searchers click on those map results.

The Map Pack is the box of three local businesses with a map that Google shows above the regular search results. Getting into those three spots is free, and it brings the highest-intent leads in local search. A homeowner who clicks on a Map Pack result is looking for someone right now.

What neglected looks like: no photos added in the last year, no posts, 6 reviews, service categories that say "contractor" instead of your specific trade. Fixing this takes a few hours and costs nothing.

3. Spending on ads without a website that converts

Picture running $3,000 a month in Google Ads and sending that traffic to a site that takes 8 seconds to load on mobile, has no click-to-call button, and buries the contact form at the bottom of a page. That's buying traffic and then fumbling it at the front door.

Before you spend a dollar on ads, your website needs to do three things in under five seconds: tell the visitor what you do, show them you're in their area, and give them an obvious way to contact you. If your site can't do those three things, your ads are expensive billboards that people glance at and forget.

4. Marketing like a big company when you're a small one

Contractors see how national brands market and try to copy it. They write corporate-sounding copy about "delivering exceptional results with a commitment to quality." They use stock photos of people in hard hats shaking hands. They build websites that look like they belong to a company with 500 employees.

Homeowners hiring a local contractor want the opposite of that. They want to know who's actually showing up to their house. They want to see photos of real work you've done, read reviews from their neighbors, and talk to a person when they call.

Your marketing advantage as a small contractor is that you're a real person who does real work in a specific area. Lean into that. Put your face on your website. Take photos on every job site. Write like you talk. The polished corporate stuff works against you because it looks impersonal.

5. No system for collecting reviews

Word of mouth used to mean your neighbor told you about their plumber over the fence. Now it means Google reviews. A contractor with 150 reviews and a 4.8 rating will usually get picked over a better contractor with 12 reviews.

The mistake isn't that contractors don't want reviews. It's that they don't have a system for asking. They finish a job, shake the customer's hand, and hope the customer goes home and writes a review, which very few people get around to doing.

We call the fix the review routine: a short text with a direct link to your Google review page right after every job, one friendly reminder a few days later, and a reply to every review that comes in. A direct link turns a review into a one-tap job, so far more customers follow through, and over a few months it adds up to a steady stream of new reviews. It costs nothing but consistency.

The pattern behind all five mistakes

Every one of these comes down to the same problem: contractors are good at the work and bad at the systems around the work. The fix for each of them is a process that runs in the background: automated follow-up, regular GBP maintenance, a site that turns visitors into calls, honest marketing, and automated review requests.

None of this is hard. It's just not what most contractors signed up to do. That's why the ones who build these systems, or hire someone to build them, end up with full calendars while their competitors wonder where the jobs went.
