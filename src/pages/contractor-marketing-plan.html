Contractor Marketing Plan Template for 2027

A marketing plan doesn't have to be a 30-page binder. For a contracting business, one page with a few real numbers and a 90-day schedule is enough to keep the phone ringing all year.

By Madeleine JolieNovember 24, 20266 min read
The short answer

A good contractor marketing plan answers three questions in order: how homeowners find you, why they choose you over the other two or three contractors they're looking at, and how fast you get them booked once they reach out. Start with a revenue goal, work backward to how many jobs and requests you need, set a budget around 7 to 8% of revenue, and spend the next 90 days fixing one stage at a time.

A lot of contractors do marketing in bursts. Work slows down, so they boost a post, pay for a month on a listing site, or print a stack of door hangers, and then they stop as soon as the schedule fills back up. The problem with that approach is that you're always starting over. A written plan, even a short one, turns marketing into a routine you can keep up during your busy season too.

The found, chosen, booked plan

The template below is built on a simple idea I call the found, chosen, booked plan. Every job you win goes through the same three stages, and a weak spot in any one of them costs you work no matter how strong the other two are.

  • Found means a homeowner who needs your trade in your area can actually see you, on Google Maps, in search results, and through referrals.
  • Chosen means that once they see you next to a couple of competitors, your reviews, photos and website give them a reason to pick you.
  • Booked means that when they call, text or fill out a form, they hear back fast and get on your calendar before they move on to the next name on the list.

Thinking in these three stages keeps you from throwing money at the wrong problem. If your phone already rings but few calls turn into jobs, buying more ads won't help, because the leak is in the booked stage. If nobody calls at all, a nicer website won't fix it on its own, because people can't find you yet.

Step 1: Set a goal you can work backward from

Skip goals like "get more work" and pick a number. The easiest way is to decide how much extra revenue you want next year, divide it by your average job, and then divide that by your close rate. That tells you how many new requests you need coming in.

Revenue goal ÷ average job = jobs you need. Jobs you need ÷ close rate = requests you need.

Say you want an extra $150,000 next year and your average job is $5,000. That's 30 more jobs. If you close about 1 out of every 4 estimates (25%), you need around 120 new requests over the year, which works out to about 10 a month. Now you have something you can actually measure your marketing against.

Step 2: Set a contractor marketing budget

The SBA guidance above is a reasonable starting point. A business doing $500,000 a year would land around $35,000 to $40,000 for marketing at 7 to 8%, or roughly $2,900 to $3,300 a month. That number covers everything, including your website, your follow-up tools, any ads and your time. If you're newer or trying to grow fast, you might spend more for a while, and if you're already booked out for months, you might spend less. There's a full breakdown in our guide to how much contractors should spend on marketing.

Picking your marketing channels

For a local contractor, the found stage usually comes down to a few channels. Your Google Business Profile is the free one that matters most, because it's what shows up in the map results when someone searches for your trade nearby. Your website backs it up, and a site with a separate page for each service and each town you work in gives Google more to match against. Google Ads can fill the gaps faster if your budget allows it, and referrals from past customers cost you nothing but a reminder. If you're paying for shared listing sites, it's worth comparing them with the alternatives to Angi and HomeAdvisor before you renew. For the free side of this, see how contractors get found on Google without ads.

The 90-day contractor marketing plan

Trying to fix everything at once is how plans die. Work through one stage per month, and by the end of the quarter all three are in place.

A simple 90-day sequence
WhenStageWhat to do
Days 1 to 30FoundFill out your Google Business Profile completely, make sure your name, address and phone match everywhere, and get a website with a page for each main service and each town you serve.
Days 31 to 60ChosenStart asking every customer for a Google review, reply to every review, and add real photos of your work and your crew to your profile and site.
Days 61 to 90BookedSet up an automatic text back for missed calls, a follow-up text for estimates that go quiet, and a simple way to book online, then review your numbers against the plan.

If your phone is already ringing and you're losing jobs after the first call, it's fine to flip the order and start with booked. The math on missed calls usually shows that it's the cheapest stage to fix.

Follow-up is part of the plan

A lot of marketing plans stop at getting the phone to ring, which leaves the most expensive leak untouched. Homeowners usually reach out to more than one contractor, and the one who answers first has the edge. An automatic reply when you can't pick up, plus one or two friendly follow-up texts on open estimates, will often win you more work than another $500 in ads. Our guides on response time and following up without being annoying go deeper on this.

Reviews and tracking

Reviews sit right in the middle of the plan because they help you get found and get chosen at the same time. Put a number on it, like four new reviews a month, and use a simple review routine so it happens after every job. For tracking, you don't need fancy software to start. Ask every new customer how they heard about you, and once a month write down how many requests, estimates and booked jobs you had. After three months you'll know which channels are pulling their weight.

The bottom line

Your 2027 marketing plan can fit on one page. Write down the goal, the budget and the numbers, then spend 90 days making sure homeowners can find you, have a reason to choose you, and get booked fast when they reach out. Keep doing those three things all year and you won't have to start from scratch every time work slows down.

What should a contractor marketing plan include?

It should include a revenue goal, your average job and close rate, a monthly budget, the channels you'll use to get found, how you'll follow up with people who reach out, how you'll collect reviews, and how you'll track where your work comes from.

How much should a contractor spend on marketing?

The SBA suggested that businesses under $5 million in revenue with 10 to 12% margins spend about 7 to 8% of revenue on marketing. Treat that as a starting point and adjust it based on how busy you are and how fast you want to grow.

What is the found, chosen, booked plan?

It's a simple way to organize contractor marketing into three stages: getting found by homeowners in your area, getting chosen over the other contractors they're comparing, and getting booked quickly once they call, text or fill out a form.

How long does it take for a contractor marketing plan to work?

Some parts, like an automatic text back for missed calls, help right away. Others, like reviews and showing up on Google, build over months, and nobody can promise a timeline for rankings. That's why the plan runs in 90-day chunks you can review and adjust.
