What Makes a Good Contractor Website? 10 Things Homeowners Look For

A lot of contractor websites look the same and never get anyone to pick up the phone. Here are the ten things homeowners actually look for before they call, and how to make sure your site has every one.

By Madeleine JolieOctober 4, 2026Updated November 24, 20268 min read
The short answer

A good contractor website makes it obvious what you do, where you work and how to reach you within a few seconds, and then gives a homeowner reasons to trust you. That means a phone number they can tap, a clear list of towns you serve, a page for each service, real photos of your work, reviews, your license and insurance, a short quote form, a fast reply when they reach out, and pages that load quickly on a phone. Everything else is decoration.

The typical contractor website has a stock photo of a guy in a hard hat, a paragraph about "quality workmanship and customer satisfaction," and a contact form buried at the bottom of a page nobody scrolls to. That kind of site isn't doing much for you, so here is what a homeowner is really looking for when they land on yours. If you'd like to score your own site as you go, our contractor website checklist covers the same ground in a printable list.

1. A phone number that's impossible to miss

A homeowner with a leaking pipe or a broken AC unit isn't going to browse your About page, because they want to talk to someone right now. Your phone number should sit at the top of every page, visible without scrolling, and it should be easy to find again once they've read a little.

On a phone, that number has to be tap-to-call. BrightLocal found that 73% of local searches start on a mobile device, and if someone has to copy your number, switch apps and paste it into their dialer, some of them simply won't bother.

2. A homepage that answers three questions in five seconds

When someone lands on your site, they need to know right away what you do, where you do it and how to contact you. That's why your headline shouldn't be "Welcome to Smith Contracting" when it could say "Roof Repair and Replacement in Tampa Bay. Call for a Free Estimate." It might sound plain, but the person with a leaky roof doesn't want clever, since all they want to know is that you can fix it and you're nearby.

This matters because people don't look for long. BrightLocal's 2026 research found that 72% of consumers look at three or fewer businesses before deciding, so your homepage has to make its case on the first screen.

3. A separate page for each service

A lot of contractors list everything they do on one page, with roof repair, new construction, gutters and siding all crammed together in a sentence each. That page has a hard time showing up on Google for anything specific, because it doesn't say much about any one job.

A dedicated page for something like "roof repair in Tampa" that explains your process, gives a rough idea of pricing and covers common problems is far more useful to a homeowner and gives Google a clear picture of what you offer. If you do five things, you should have five service pages, each with its own title, its own description and enough real content to help someone who's researching that service. There's more on this in our post on why contractor websites need more than one page.

4. A clear service area

A homeowner in a nearby town wants to know whether you'll even come out to them, and if your site only says "serving the greater area," they have to guess. List the cities and neighborhoods you cover on your homepage and in your footer, and give your main towns their own pages that mention the work you do there.

This helps with Google too. Google says local results depend mainly on relevance, distance and prominence, and spelling out where you work makes it easier for Google to connect you with searches in those places.

5. Photos of your actual work

Real photos are the easiest way to stand out from the other contractors in your market. Homeowners can tell the difference between a stock photo and a picture you took on a job site, and the real one wins because it's proof that you do the work.

Before and after photos work especially well, like a rotted deck next to the new one you built or a bathroom mid-demo next to the finished tile. Taking a few photos on every job only takes a minute, and over a few months you'll have a gallery that sells for you.

6. Reviews on the site, not just on Google

Your Google reviews help you show up in the map results, but reviews on your own website matter once someone clicks through and starts comparing. If a homeowner lands on your site and sees a stack of five-star reviews from people in their area, you've answered their biggest question before they've asked it.

A live review feed that pulls from Google works best because it updates itself and shows real names, dates and star ratings. Static testimonials with no name or date can look made up, even when they aren't. If you need more reviews to show, here's how to get more Google reviews without breaking Google's rules.

7. Your license, insurance and warranty

Letting a stranger work on your home takes trust, and homeowners want to know you're licensed and insured before they let you through the door. Put your license number, a line saying you're insured, and any warranty you offer on your workmanship somewhere easy to find, like your footer and your About page.

In many states a homeowner can look up a contractor's license online through the state licensing board, so a license number they can check carries more weight than the words "licensed and insured" on their own. It's a small detail that separates you from contractors who leave it off.

8. A quote form that asks the right questions

A form that only asks for a name, email and message doesn't work well for a contractor, because the homeowner doesn't know what to put in the message box and you end up with requests that tell you nothing.

A better form asks what service they need, their zip code and when they need it done, along with a phone number. Keep it short, since every extra question gives someone a reason to quit halfway, and when you call back you'll already know what they want and sound prepared.

9. A fast reply when someone reaches out

Your website can do everything right and still lose the job if nobody answers. A homeowner who calls while you're on a roof, or fills out your form at 9 PM, is probably contacting other contractors too, and the first one to reply has a real head start. CallRail's 2025 survey found that 78% of consumers have given up on a business after an unanswered call.

The simplest fix is an automatic text that goes out within seconds of a missed call or a new form request, so the homeowner knows a real business got their message while you finish the job you're on. Our guide to missed-call text-back explains how it works, and our post on why the first text wins the job covers why speed matters so much.

10. Fast pages on a phone

Your site can look incredible on a big monitor, but if it takes four seconds to load on a phone, you've already lost a lot of your visitors. Think with Google reports that 53% of mobile visits are abandoned when a page takes longer than three seconds to load.

Compress your images, skip the auto-playing video on the homepage, and test your site at Google's PageSpeed Insights with a goal of scoring above 80 on mobile.

The sites that actually book jobs

The best contractor websites aren't flashy. They load fast, they make it obvious what you do and where, they show real photos, real reviews and proof that you're licensed, and they make it easy to call or ask for a quote, with a quick reply waiting on the other end. If your site is missing a few of these, start with the phone number and the reply, because those two affect every single visitor.

What should be on a contractor's homepage?

Your homepage should say what you do and where in the headline, show a tap-to-call phone number near the top, and include your service list, real job photos, reviews and a short quote form. A homeowner should understand all of that within a few seconds.

How many pages should a contractor website have?

There's no exact number, but a good starting point is a homepage, one page for each service you offer, a page for each main town you cover, an About page and a contact page. A one-page site has a hard time showing up for specific searches.

Should I put my license number on my website?

It's a good idea. Homeowners want to know you're licensed and insured, and in many states they can look up a license number online, so showing it gives them a quick way to check you out.

How fast should a contractor website load?

Aim for pages that load in under three seconds on a phone. Think with Google reports that 53% of mobile visits are abandoned when a page takes longer than that.
