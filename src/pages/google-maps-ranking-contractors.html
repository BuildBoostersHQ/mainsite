The Contractor's Guide to Ranking on Google Maps

Google tells you exactly what it looks at when it picks the businesses in the map results. Here is what that means for a contractor, and a simple check you can run on your own profile this week.

By Madeleine JolieNovember 24, 20266 min read
The short answer

Google says its local results are based mainly on relevance, distance and prominence. For a contractor, that means your Google Business Profile should clearly match the work people search for, your location and service areas should be accurate, and your business should look established and trusted online, with steady reviews, real photos and a website that backs it all up. Nobody can pay Google for a better map ranking, so anyone who guarantees you the top spot is guessing.

When a homeowner searches for "roof repair near me" or "emergency plumber," the first thing they usually see is a map with a short list of businesses under it. That box is called the map pack, and getting into it is one of the best ways for a contractor to get found without paying for ads. The good news is that you don't have to guess how it works, because Google has published the basics in its own help pages.

How Google Maps ranking works for contractors

According to Google Business Profile Help, local results are based mainly on three things. Relevance is how well your profile matches what someone searched for. Distance is how far your business is from the person searching, or from the location they typed into their search. Prominence is how well known and trusted your business is, which Google says can include things like reviews and links from around the web.

Google is also clear about what doesn't work. It says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking, which is worth remembering the next time someone calls promising you the number one spot on Maps.

The three-part map pack check

To make Google's three factors easy to act on, I use something I call the three-part map pack check. It is simply a set of questions, one for each factor, that you ask about your own Google Business Profile and website. A relevance question asks whether Google can tell what you do, a distance question asks whether Google knows where you are and where you work, and a prominence question asks whether your business looks established and trusted compared to the other contractors around you.

The three-part map pack check
PartWhat Google looks atWhat you can check
RelevanceHow well your profile matches the searchYour primary category, your services list, your description and the service pages on your website
DistanceHow far you are from the searcherYour verified address or service areas, and whether they match where you actually work

Part 1: Relevance, or does Google know what you do?

Relevance is the part you have the most control over, and it starts with your categories. Your primary category should be the trade you most want to be found for, like Roofing contractor or Plumber, and your secondary categories should cover the other work you really do. After that, fill in your services list with the specific jobs you take on, because a homeowner searching for "water heater installation" is looking for exactly that phrase.

Your website plays a big part here as well. A site with one page that lists everything you do gives Google very little to work with, while a site with a separate page for each main service gives it a clear signal for each one. If you want to see why that matters, read why your contractor website needs more than one page.

One thing to avoid is stuffing keywords into your business name. Google's guidelines ask you to use your real business name, the one on your truck and your invoices, and adding extra words like "Best Roofer Miami" can get your profile suspended.

Part 2: Distance, or does Google know where you work?

You can't move your shop closer to every person who searches, so distance is mostly about being accurate. If customers come to your office, your verified address should be the real one. If you go to your customers, which is how a lot of contractors work, you can set your profile up as a service-area business, hide your street address and list the cities or areas you serve.

Keep your service areas honest. Listing a huge region that you don't really cover won't make Google show you everywhere, and it can bring in calls from places you don't want to drive to. Service area pages on your website, like the ones on our Miami contractor website page, help back up where you work with real content for each town.

A virtual office or a P.O. box doesn't count as a business address under Google's guidelines, so don't use one to try to show up in a city where you don't have a real location.

Part 3: Prominence, or does your business look trusted?

Prominence is where steady habits pay off. Google says that more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking, and it also says that replying to reviews shows customers you value their feedback. The simplest way to build this is to ask every customer for a review right after the job is done, which we cover step by step in how to get more Google reviews.

Photos help too, because Google encourages businesses to show their work with photos and videos, and homeowners want to see real jobs and real trucks instead of stock pictures. Our guide to contractor website photos covers what to shoot and how to use it. Beyond your profile, a solid website, accurate listings on other sites and mentions from local organizations all help Google understand that your business is real and active.

Common Google Maps mistakes contractors make

  • Leaving the profile half finished, even though Google says businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up
  • Choosing a broad primary category when a more specific one fits the work better
  • Letting months go by without a new review, a new photo or a reply to a customer
  • Paying someone who promises a guaranteed ranking, when Google says ranking can't be bought
  • Linking the profile to a website that is slow, hard to use on a phone or missing your main services

What to do after you show up

Showing up in the map pack only matters if people actually call and you actually answer. When a homeowner taps your listing, they will look at your reviews, tap through to your website and then call or text. If that call goes unanswered, a lot of people simply move on to the next business in the list, which is why we wrote about what missed calls cost contractors.

The bottom line

Google Maps ranking isn't a secret, and it isn't something you can buy. Run the three-part map pack check once a month, fix anything that comes up in relevance, distance or prominence, and keep asking for reviews after every job, because those steady habits are what Google says it looks for.

How do I get my contracting business on Google Maps?

Create a free Google Business Profile, verify it, choose the category that best matches your trade, and fill in your services, hours, service areas and photos. Once it is verified, your business can appear in Google Maps and the map results in search.

Can I pay Google to rank higher on Maps?

Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. You can pay for ads that appear near the map results, but those are labeled as ads and don't change where your profile ranks on its own.

How long does it take to show up in the map pack?

There is no set timeline, and nobody can honestly promise one. It depends on how competitive your trade and area are, how complete your profile is, and how your reviews and website compare to the other contractors near the person searching.

Do I need a website to rank on Google Maps?

You can have a Google Business Profile without a website, but a good website gives Google more information about your services and areas, and it gives homeowners a place to check you out before they call.

Why does my ranking change depending on where I search from?

Distance is one of Google's three main factors, so results change with the location of the person searching. Checking from your shop will not show you what a customer across town sees.
