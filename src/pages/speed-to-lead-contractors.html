Speed-to-Lead for Contractors: Why the First Text Wins the Job

When a homeowner reaches out to three contractors at once, the first one to reply has a real head start. Here's why a fast first text matters more than your ad budget, and how to send one even when you're up on a roof.

By Madeleine JolieSeptember 28, 2026Updated November 24, 20266 min read
The short answer

Speed-to-lead is how fast you reply after a homeowner calls, fills out your form or sends a message, and for contractors the first reply is often the one that wins the job. Since you can't always pick up, the fastest way to reply is an automatic text that goes out within seconds, tells them a real business got their request and keeps them from calling the next name on the list. Follow it with a real call once you're off the job.

A lot of contractors put serious money into ads and still wonder why the phone rings but the jobs don't close. Often the problem isn't the ad at all, because the real trouble is what happens in the first few minutes after someone calls or fills out the form.

The research on this is old but clear. In a Harvard Business Review study, companies that answered a web inquiry within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to reach the decision maker as companies that waited even one more hour.

Why the first text wins the job

Picture a homeowner who needs a roof looked at. They search "roofer near me," tap the first three results and call or send a request to each one in about five minutes. If one contractor texts back within a minute and the other two get back to them at lunch, the homeowner is already talking to the first one, and there's a good chance the estimate gets booked right there.

That's how people actually shop for local services. In BrightLocal's 2026 research, 72% of consumers looked at three or fewer businesses before deciding, and 75% made up their mind in under 30 minutes. Waiting for a voicemail doesn't help much either, because Quo's call data shows only about 15% of callers leave one.

A text is the best first reply because it reaches the homeowner right away, they can read it whenever they glance at their phone, and you don't have to stop working to send it when it goes out automatically.

The math behind response time

Here's a hypothetical example to show how it works. Say you spend $1,500 a month on ads, that brings in 60 calls and form requests, and you book 8 of them, which works out to about $187 in ad spend for each booked job. If replying faster helped you book 12 of those same 60 requests, your cost per booked job would drop to $125 without spending another dollar on advertising.

The same thinking applies to missed calls. The math we use is missed calls per week × 52 × your close rate × your average job, so 3 missed calls a week at a 20% close rate and a $1,500 average job adds up to $46,800 a year. You can plug in your own numbers with our missed-call calculator.

What the first text should say

You're on a roof, under a house or driving between jobs, so you can't answer every call the second it comes in. That's why the first reply should go out on its own. With missed-call text-back, anyone who calls and doesn't reach you gets an automatic text within seconds, and new form requests can get the same kind of reply. Something like this works well:

Hi, this is Mike with Ruiz Roofing. Sorry I missed your call, I'm on a job right now. What can I help you with? I'll call you back as soon as I'm off the roof.

That text confirms they reached a real business, starts the conversation before anyone else does, and buys you time to call back properly. For more wording ideas, see our 5 texts to send after a missed call.

Why "I'll call them tonight" doesn't work

Writing down names and calling everyone back after work feels organized, but by 7 PM a homeowner who needed help that morning has usually found someone else or stopped thinking about it. The good news is that the bar is low, since the companies in the Harvard Business Review study took 42 hours on average to respond and 23% never responded at all, so even a quick automatic text puts you ahead of a lot of businesses.

What to do right now

Start by testing your own response time. Have a friend call your business line while you're busy and fill out your website form, then time how long it takes for them to hear back. If the honest answer is whenever you get around to it, set up an automatic first text for missed calls and form requests, then follow up with a real call once you're off the job. What does speed-to-lead mean for a contractor?

It's how quickly you reply after a homeowner calls, fills out your form or messages you. The faster that first reply, the better your chance of being the contractor they end up talking to.

How fast should a contractor reply to a new request?

Aim for an automatic text within seconds of a missed call and within a minute of a form request, then a real call the same day. Homeowners often decide within half an hour, so waiting until the evening can cost you the job.

Is it better to text or call first?

Text first when you can't pick up, because a text reaches them right away and they can read it whenever they look at their phone. Then call once you're free. There's more on this in our post on texting vs. calling customers.

Will an automatic text feel impersonal to customers?

It doesn't have to. A short text with your name and business name that says you're on a job and will call back soon reads like a real person, because it's exactly what you would say if you had a free hand.
